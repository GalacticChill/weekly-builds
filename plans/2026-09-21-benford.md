# Week of 2026-09-21 — Benford's Law

**Theme:** data/statistics — a self-contained forensic-statistics capstone.
**Type:** a new technique (distributional goodness-of-fit); user delegated ("do the
next one"), so chosen for standalone merit rather than personal direction.

## The question
Do real datasets really lead with the digit 1 ~30% of the time (Benford's Law)? Why?
And how good is the leading digit, honestly, as a detector of fabricated numbers?

## Scope (shipped)
- `law.py` — leading-digit extraction (scale/sign invariant, drops 0 & non-finite),
  Benford probabilities `log10(1+1/d)`, and conformity via BOTH chi-square (+p-value)
  and Nigrini MAD bands. `analyze` -> BenfordResult.
- `datasets.py` — the test suite: real World Bank figures (population, GDP, land
  area, CO2; fetched + cached to `data/worldbank.csv`), mathematical positive
  controls (Fibonacci, powers of two), and negative controls (uniform noise, a
  bounded normal ~ heights).
- `plots.py` — single-dataset observed-vs-Benford bars, and a small-multiple grid
  (blue = conforms, red = doesn't).
- CLI + committed data snapshot.
- 13 offline tests (known-answer: Benford endpoints 30.1%/4.6%, digit extraction,
  Fibonacci/powers conform, uniform/bounded don't, conformity bands).

## Findings
- **Real WB data conforms:** pooled 1,019 values MAD 0.005 ("close"), first-digit-1
  share 29.6% vs Benford's 30.1%; per-indicator sets "acceptable/marginal".
- **Positive controls near-exact:** Fibonacci & powers of two MAD 0.001.
- **Negative controls fail as they should:** uniform random flat (1-share 11.6%,
  MAD 0.060); bounded heights lead with 1 ~99.9% (MAD 0.155) — a vivid illustration
  that Benford needs data spanning many orders of magnitude.

## The honest core
The point is the caveats: conformity != honesty, nonconformity != fraud (bounded or
assigned numbers fail innocently; small n is noisy; chi-square rejects everything at
large n, which is why forensic accounting uses MAD bands and why the repo shows
both). Benford is a genuine regularity and a useful screen, never a verdict —
matches the portfolio's recurring "don't fool yourself" theme.

## Why this matters for the portfolio story
Adds a new technique (goodness-of-fit / forensic statistics), is memorable and
